## Premise

Moss, a tunnel-born badger courier with illegal wetware whiskers, runs contraband data through layered city sprawl. A dub-infused free colony hijacks pirate radio channels and recruits Moss for a chain of heists. Each heist exposes a corporation's orbital control network. By the end, Moss helps convert a captured satellite asteroid into a rebel broadcast fortress.

The story is built as a five-act Brechtian drama: each act exposes a social machine, names its price, then asks the player whether liberation is possible without repeating the old machine in a new costume.

## Five-act story arc

### Prologue — The Song of the Toll

Projected placard: **A city that charges for crossing the street will one day charge for breathing.**

Moss is introduced as a courier who survives by speed, not politics. The first run through the Lower Sprawl looks like a simple theft: steal a wafer-key from a toll office. But the key reveals a ledger linking street gates, water meters, elevator permissions, and orbital air leases. The first enemy institution is not a villain's castle but a turnstile.

The dub colony appears as a pirate signal, not yet a place. Its song names the system before Moss understands it.

### Act I — The Badger Sells His Feet

Worlds: Lower Sprawl, Chrome Arcology.

Moss learns that every route is owned. In the sprawl, children crawl through drainpipes because walking across bridges costs money. In the arcology, polished elevators float above hidden labor floors. Rook Null joins as a companion and explains logistics with disturbing calm.

Dramatic conflict: Moss wants to steal enough to escape. Sister Version wants to turn every theft into public proof. Lio warns Moss that rebellion gets families punished.

Act turn: Moss steals the elevator seed and sees prisoners moved as cargo. The personal job becomes a public crime scene.

### Act II — Treason at the Mirror Banquet

World: Straylight Mirage.

The party reaches orbital luxury. Everything is reflective, rehearsed, and fake. Banquet dialogue alternates with duels; bosses speak in etiquette and contracts. Auntie Subharmonic recognizes an old revolutionary now working as a court performer.

Lio betrays Moss, but the betrayal is staged as a lesson: they did not switch sides because they stopped caring. They switched because Director Vane bought the debt contract on Lio's family and turned love into a leash.

Act turn: the player chooses whether to expose Lio publicly, protect them, or use the betrayal as bait. This choice affects trust, later companion availability, and whether the rebellion becomes merciful or punitive.

### Act III — The Colony Teaches the Price of Air

Worlds: Dub Colony, Uplink Barrens.

The dub colony becomes the moving home base: greenhouse cars, studio temples, repair decks, speaker gardens, solar sails. The colony argues with itself. Some want to stay hidden. Some want open revolt. King Feedback believes safety requires central command. Auntie Subharmonic insists that a free colony must remain free even when afraid.

Naya Root joins as a computer-controlled shield fighter. The player learns that companions are not upgrades; they are political relationships with trust, disagreement, and breaking points.

In the Uplink Barrens, the group hunts the debt ledger shards. The Black-Ice Fox turns hacking into a duel, teaching that code is not a minigame; it is combat state.

Act turn: the colony votes. The player's actions determine whether the colony enters the rebellion as a chorus, an army, or a frightened supplier.

### Act IV — The Old Ally Wears a New Uniform

World: Orbital Lift.

The rebellion climbs the machine that carries obedience: cargo containers, sky cables, customs gates, counterweights, and prayer-like logistics schedules. The Elevator Angel is not hateful. It is obedient. It asks Moss: if a machine feeds a cruel order, is the machine guilty, or only useful?

Lio returns in a new uniform, wounded and ashamed, either as enemy, hostage, or fragile ally depending on earlier choices. Murr Murrby appears inside a customs scanner with a full shop and says: “Emergency prices are immoral. Fortunately, morality is discounted today.”

Act turn: Moss reverses the cargo flow and frees prisoners, but doing so also alerts Director Vane that the rebellion can seize orbit.

### Act V — The Asteroid Learns to Speak

Worlds: Asteroid Redoubt, Final Broadcast.

The rebels seize a satellite asteroid and turn it into a transmitter fortress. The final conflict is not only a duel with Director Vane. It is a fight over authorship: who writes the ledger, who owns air, who names debt, who gets to broadcast truth.

Treason becomes collective. The final traitor is not one person but a faction inside the rebellion tempted to replace Vane's sky-lock with their own command lock. Former enemies return as arguments: King Feedback's safety, Madame Vitrine's contract logic, the Elevator Angel's obedience, the Black-Ice Fox's cynicism, Lio's fear.

Final movement: Moss must fight, hack, and answer. The last action is not just an attack; it is choosing what the broadcast says.

## Mission pattern

```text
placard / song
  -> briefing at dub colony or local safehouse
  -> choose route risk and companion
  -> enter side-scrolling level
  -> platforming-combat room
  -> optional code gate or dialogue route
  -> merchant / ally / political interruption
  -> 1-3 sub-bosses revealing the institution
  -> heist room puzzle
  -> alarm escape
  -> endboss or act argument
  -> colony debrief, trust shifts, skill unlocks
```

## Dialogue design promise

Classic RPG text boxes should be large, readable, and theatrical. Choices do not merely select flavor; they change trust, heat, merchant prices, companion availability, and whether the rebellion is remembered as mutual aid or replacement authority.
